# Silent Witch: The Girl Who Found Her Voice in Silence

_First impressions — Episodes 1–2_

After finishing _Code Geass_, I wanted something different. Something a little more relaxed, perhaps a romance, or just an anime I could watch without feeling like the fate of an entire nation depended on what happened in the next twenty minutes.

Naturally, I ignored every recommendation I received and started _Secrets of the Silent Witch_.

And damn, the first two episodes were interesting.

Not in the same way _Code Geass_ was interesting, though. That show had a particular talent for making me feel like I absolutely **had** to watch the next episode. Every revelation, every tactical maneuver, every cliffhanger demanded an immediate continuation.

_Silent Witch_ hasn't given me that feeling.

Instead, it's done something quieter.

It's introduced just enough of its world, its politics, and—most importantly—its characters to make me curious.

I'm not desperately asking what happens next.

I'm asking **why Monica Everett is the way she is.**

And after only two episodes, that's already a compelling reason to keep watching.

## The Girl Behind the Legend

Our introduction to Monica is almost absurd.

She's awkward. She's easily overwhelmed. She can barely survive a normal social interaction. The simplest conversation seems capable of sending her into a complete internal crisis.

She's also rather adorable in how utterly derpy she can be.

And then we discover that this girl is the **Silent Witch**, one of the Seven Sages and an extraordinarily accomplished mage.

The contrast is hilarious.

Here's someone who has achieved something exceptional in a world built around magic, yet she appears incapable of comfortably existing in ordinary society.

At first, this could easily have been a straightforward character gimmick: an overpowered protagonist who happens to be comically shy.

But the show doesn't stop there.

It introduces her fellow sage, who promptly decides that Monica needs to leave the comfort of her secluded life and undertake a politically sensitive assignment.

And the way he handles her is comedy gold.

**THE KING OF HELL.**

What a ridiculous dynamic.

He knows exactly how difficult Monica can be, and rather than patiently trying to convince her through ordinary means, he resorts to the sort of manipulation that seems most likely to get the job done.

I don't necessarily read this as some elaborate attempt to help Monica overcome her problems.

He has a job that needs doing, he knows his friend, and he's apparently decided that finding the most effective way to push her into action is preferable to dealing with the alternatives.

It's such a funny relationship because Monica is simultaneously an extraordinary mage and someone who can be handled like a stubborn child refusing to leave her room.

And yet, beneath the comedy, there's something more interesting going on.

The contrast between Monica's reputation and her personality isn't simply there to make us laugh.

It makes us wonder how someone like her became the Silent Witch in the first place.

## The Flashback That Changes Everything

For me, the most fascinating moment in these first two episodes is the glimpse we get of Monica's past.

Because the young Monica we see is so different from the girl we've been following.

There's an expressiveness to her that seems almost impossible to reconcile with the present-day Monica.

It's such a dramatic contrast that the question practically asks itself:

**What happened to her?**

And the show gives us just enough of an answer to make that question even more painful.

We glimpse the trauma behind her behavior, but we don't receive the complete story.

That's what makes the reveal so effective.

It doesn't merely introduce a mystery about Monica's past. It changes how we understand everything we've already seen.

Her difficulty speaking to people no longer feels like a simple matter of being shy or lacking confidence.

**It's trauma.**

And that's an important distinction.

Someone who lacks confidence might need encouragement, experience, or reassurance.

Someone whose behavior has been shaped by a deeply scarring experience is dealing with something much more complicated.

Monica isn't simply afraid that a conversation will go badly.

Something has happened that makes the very act of interacting with other people frightening.

We don't yet know the full circumstances, and I don't want to pretend we do. We've only watched two episodes.

But we have seen enough to understand that the girl we meet at the beginning of the story is not necessarily the girl Monica has always been.

And that makes her character immediately more compelling.

The show has given us a before and an after without fully explaining the distance between them.

I want to understand what happened in between.

## Silence as an Act of Will

What makes Monica particularly fascinating is that her trauma doesn't exist separately from her greatest accomplishment.

It seems intimately connected to it.

Monica's difficulty with speaking created an extraordinary obstacle for someone who wanted to practice magic in a world where incantations matter.

But rather than abandon magic, she pursued something seemingly impossible.

**She learned to cast spells without speaking.**

And I think the sheer audacity of that deserves more attention than it initially receives.

Imagine being so deeply affected by an experience that speaking itself becomes an obstacle, yet still possessing enough determination to challenge the established conventions of magic.

That's an incredible act of will.

Monica didn't simply discover a convenient alternative.

She had to find a way to continue doing something important to her despite a limitation that might have stopped someone else entirely.

And that completely changes how I view her.

It's easy to look at Monica's awkwardness and assume she's weak, timid, or incapable of confronting difficulties.

But that interpretation falls apart when we consider what she's accomplished.

**Monica isn't someone who lacks courage.**

She's someone whose courage manifests in ways that aren't immediately obvious.

She may struggle to speak to a single person, but she has the determination to accomplish something that earned her a place among the most exceptional mages in the world.

Those two things can coexist.

In fact, their coexistence is precisely what makes her interesting.

And there's something almost poetic about her title.

The world knows her as the _Silent Witch_ because she can perform extraordinary magic without an incantation.

But for Monica, silence appears to carry a much more personal meaning.

**What the world recognizes as genius may have emerged from one girl's determination to survive and continue doing what she loved.**

I don't think that makes the trauma itself a good thing. Her suffering doesn't become worthwhile merely because something remarkable came out of it.

But it does mean her achievement represents more than technical brilliance.

It represents her refusal to give up.

## Mathematics: The Place Monica Can Breathe

Another thing I love about Monica is how naturally she retreats into mathematics.

It's not merely something she's good at.

It's something she seems comfortable with in a way she isn't comfortable with people.

When she encounters a social situation, she becomes hesitant and overwhelmed.

But when she can understand something through numbers, her entire way of engaging with it changes.

And the funniest possible example of this is, of course, the prince.

Monica sees this ridiculously beautiful man and promptly faints.

**SHE ACTUALLY FAINTS FROM BEAUTY.**

I cannot take this girl seriously. 😭

But then, rather than simply reacting to him like a conventional romance protagonist, she starts thinking about the proportions of his body and how they relate to the golden ratio.

MONICA.

YOU ARE SUPPOSED TO BE PROCESSING YOUR FEELINGS.

NOT CONDUCTING A MATHEMATICAL INVESTIGATION INTO WHY THIS MAN IS ATTRACTIVE.

And yet, that's exactly why the scene works.

Her reaction is uniquely hers.

She doesn't suddenly become a different person because she's attracted to someone.

She remains the same mathematically obsessed Monica, attempting to understand something emotionally overwhelming through a framework she knows.

It's comedy, but it's also characterization.

Mathematics seems to provide her with a kind of structure that human interactions don't.

Numbers can be studied. Patterns can be recognized. Problems can be worked through.

People are much less predictable.

And perhaps that's why she returns to mathematics so naturally.

It's familiar.

It's something she understands.

It's somewhere she can breathe.

Of course, I don't want to reduce her love of mathematics entirely to trauma. She may simply love the subject, and that passion deserves to exist independently of whatever happened to her.

But the way she retreats into it when confronted with overwhelming experiences is worth noticing.

And if this show really does develop a romance between Monica and the prince, I hope it preserves that aspect of her.

I don't want her to suddenly stop being the girl who analyzes beauty through mathematics.

That's part of what makes her Monica.

Also, marrying the prince would certainly be one way to ensure his protection.

Just saying. 😏

## Small Conversations, Significant Steps

One of the things I appreciate about these episodes is that Monica's development isn't presented through some dramatic transformation.

She's not suddenly cured of her fear because someone tells her to believe in herself.

She's still uncomfortable.

She's still struggling.

But we can already see small moments in which she begins interacting with the people around her.

I particularly liked her interaction with the girl who helps tie her hair.

It's a relatively ordinary moment, but that ordinariness is what makes it meaningful.

Monica manages to speak a little more.

She participates in an interaction that doesn't immediately collapse into complete panic.

And the girl herself provides an interesting contrast.

She seems capable of dealing with gossip and other people's judgments without allowing them to completely overwhelm her.

Monica, meanwhile, struggles to survive even a single conversation.

There's also something amusingly tsundere about the girl's behavior.

The contrast is wonderful.

One girl has enough composure to maintain a prickly attitude toward the people around her.

The other is fighting for her life just to get through a social exchange.

But I don't think the point is that Monica suddenly becomes comfortable around kind people.

She doesn't.

Even gentler interactions are difficult for her.

Perhaps she's a little more relaxed around people who treat her well, or people she already knows and trusts, but her fear doesn't simply disappear.

And that's a good thing for the writing.

**Progress doesn't have to mean that the problem is gone.**

Sometimes progress is simply managing a conversation that would previously have felt impossible.

Monica may have entered this situation because she was forced out of her comfort zone, but that doesn't mean every connection she makes has to be unpleasant.

She can be frightened of interacting with people and still find moments of genuine enjoyment in those interactions.

Both feelings can exist at once.

I also find myself wondering about the possibility of romance.

If the prince eventually becomes someone who understands Monica's trauma and helps her heal, I could see that becoming a genuinely touching relationship.

But I'd want it to be more than a story about a handsome prince arriving to fix a broken girl.

Monica is already an accomplished person with her own interests, abilities, and determination.

**She doesn't need someone to give her courage. She's already shown us she has it.**

What she may need is someone who understands why she's afraid and gives her room to feel safe.

And if that person happens to be the man whose face is apparently powerful enough to knock her unconscious, well...

That's going to be an entertaining romance.

## A World That Invites Curiosity

I've spent most of this article discussing Monica because she's easily the most interesting part of the show so far.

But I also want to give credit to the worldbuilding.

The political setting is gradually taking shape without overwhelming the story.

We have the Seven Sages, the reputation attached to Monica's title, the prince she has been assigned to protect, and the suggestion that the world of magic is closely connected to political power.

There's enough information to understand that Monica's assignment matters, but not so much that the first two episodes become an extended explanation of institutions and factions.

I like that balance.

The political intrigue gives the story a direction.

Monica gives it a heart.

And the comedy keeps everything from becoming too heavy.

The King of Hell's interactions with Monica are consistently entertaining, and Nero is an absolute delight.

I particularly enjoy how these characters prevent the show from treating Monica's extraordinary reputation with constant solemnity.

Yes, she's the Silent Witch.

Yes, she's an exceptional mage.

But she's also Monica, and the people around her aren't going to let her forget it.

That combination makes the world feel welcoming rather than intimidating.

There's mystery here, but not the exhausting kind where every scene seems designed to produce another cliffhanger.

Instead, the show is slowly introducing a place I want to spend more time in.

## Curiosity Is Enough

I think that's the biggest difference between my experience with _Silent Witch_ and something like _Code Geass_.

With _Code Geass_, I was constantly chasing the next revelation.

I needed to know what Lelouch would do, how his plans would unfold, and what consequences would follow.

The tension was almost addictive.

_Silent Witch_ hasn't produced that same urgency.

And I don't think it needs to.

After two episodes, I'm interested in Monica's past, the nature of her trauma, the relationships she's beginning to form, and the political situation surrounding her assignment.

I'm curious about how her extraordinary magical abilities will interact with a world that requires her to do something far more frightening than cast a spell:

**Talk to people.**

But most importantly, I want to see Monica grow without losing the qualities that make her so endearing.

I don't want her mathematical obsession to disappear.

I don't want her awkwardness to be treated as something inherently wrong with her.

And I certainly don't want the show to suggest that her trauma can be solved by simply forcing her through enough uncomfortable situations.

What I want is to understand her.

I want to see what happened to the expressive young girl we glimpsed in that flashback.

I want to see whether the people around her can become people she genuinely feels safe with.

And I want to see how someone who has already demonstrated such extraordinary willpower learns to navigate a world that doesn't operate according to the rules of mathematics.

Because that's the contradiction at the center of Monica Everett.

**She's capable of performing magic without speaking, yet speaking to another person may be the most difficult thing she does.**

And somehow, after only two episodes, both sides of that contradiction feel equally important.

I don't know yet whether _Silent Witch_ will become something exceptional.

Two episodes aren't enough to make that judgment.

But they've given me a character I want to understand, a world I want to explore, and a story I'm happy to spend time with.

Sometimes, that's all a first impression needs.

**Monica Everett doesn't need to discover that she's brave. She's already proven it.**

**What I want to see is whether she can discover a world in which she no longer has to be afraid.**
